Food is bought with the eyes. **Food packaging design** has to make the product look delicious, signal quality and price, protect what's inside and carry a lot of mandatory information, all on a small surface that may be seen on a shelf, in a delivery app or in someone's hand at home. This guide covers what makes food packaging work and what to plan for before you print.

## Appetite appeal

Show the food at its best: rich photography, honest illustrations or a window onto the real product. Avoid stock images that look like every competitor.

- **Photography** works when the food itself is the hero, such as snacks, ready meals or bakery products. Invest in a proper food shoot; poor photography makes good food look cheap.
- **Illustration** suits products where ingredients or origin tell the story, like teas, spices and spreads, and it helps premium ranges stand apart from photo-heavy shelves.
- **Windows** build trust by showing the real product, but they need careful structural design and the product has to look good after transport.

## Make the category and flavour instantly clear

Shoppers spend seconds on a shelf. They need to know in a glance what the product is, which flavour or variant it is, and who it's for.

| Approach | Example use |
|---|---|
| Colour per flavour | Snacks and beverages |
| Ingredient illustration | Spreads, sauces, teas |
| Pattern per variant | Premium ranges |

Build these choices into a **range system**: fixed positions for the brand, product name and key claims, and a flexible zone for flavour colour and imagery. New variants can then be added quickly without redesigning everything.

## Signal quality and price

Packaging tells shoppers where you sit in the market before they read anything. Matte finishes, restrained colour and generous white space tend to read as premium; bold colour and big claims tend to read as value and energy. Neither is better: what matters is that the cues match your price and audience.

## Practical details

- Resealable closures for multi-use packs
- Clear cooking or serving instructions
- Space for batch codes and dates
- Legible mandatory information: ingredients, nutrition, allergens, veg/non-veg marks and manufacturer details, laid out according to current FSSAI labelling rules
- Material that protects freshness and survives transport and storage

Mandatory information is often left until the end of a project and then squeezed in. Plan its space in the layout from the first concept.

## Online and delivery

On apps, packs appear tiny. Test designs at thumbnail size, and prepare listing images showing serving suggestions and pack size. Make sure the brand and flavour are readable when the pack is only a few centimetres tall on a phone screen.

## Sustainability

Many shoppers now look for recyclable or reduced packaging. Be specific and honest in any environmental claims, and check that the materials you choose actually work for your product's shelf life.

## Plan for printing

- Confirm the print process and material with your printer before final artwork
- Get physical proofs and check colour, especially for food photography
- Check every piece of text with a second person; errors on food labels are costly to fix after printing

## Choosing a food packaging design agency

Look for food category experience, print knowledge and range thinking. Ask to see packs that have actually been printed and sold, not just mockups.
