As companies grow from one product to many, a question appears: should each new product have its own name and look, or should everything carry the main brand? That's **brand architecture**, the way a company organises its brands, sub-brands and products so customers understand how they relate. Getting it right makes launches cheaper and the portfolio clearer; getting it wrong leads to confused customers and marketing budgets spread too thin.

## The main models

| Model | How it works | Best when |
|---|---|---|
| Branded house | One master brand, descriptive product names | Products share an audience and promise |
| Endorsed brands | Distinct product brands "by" the parent | New audiences, but parent adds trust |
| Sub-brands | Product names with their own identity within the master | Ranges need personality |
| House of brands | Separate brands, parent mostly invisible | Very different audiences or price points |

## Branded house

Everything carries the master brand, and products have descriptive names. Every launch builds the same brand, and marketing spend works harder. The risk: a problem with one product can affect the whole brand, and it's harder to stretch into very different markets.

## Endorsed brands

Each product line has its own name and identity, but is visibly backed by the parent brand. This lets you reach a new audience or price point while borrowing the parent's trust. It costs more than a branded house because each endorsed brand needs some of its own marketing.

## Sub-brands

A middle ground: the master brand stays dominant, but a range gets its own name and personality, such as a premium line or a kids' range. Sub-brands are useful when one part of the portfolio needs to feel different without becoming a separate company.

## House of brands

Separate brands for separate markets, with the parent company mostly invisible to customers. It allows brands to target very different audiences and protects each from the others' problems, but it's the most expensive model, because every brand has to be built from scratch.

## Questions to decide

1. Does the new product serve the same customer?
2. Could it hurt the main brand if it fails?
3. Will it be sold in different channels or at a different price?
4. Can you afford to market a separate brand?

If the answers are "yes, no, no, no", a branded house is usually right. The more the answers lean the other way, the more separation makes sense.

## Signs your architecture needs attention

- Customers don't realise your products come from the same company
- Every launch has a different logo style and naming approach
- Your website navigation is confusing because brands and products overlap
- Marketing budget is spread across too many names to build any of them

## Packaging and ranges

Architecture shows up most on packaging: how variants, flavours and lines are distinguished while still looking like one family. Decide what stays fixed across all products (usually the master logo, its position and a core colour or typeface) and what each line can change.

## Naming

Your architecture shapes naming. In a branded house, product names should be descriptive and simple. With sub-brands or separate brands, names need to be distinctive and checked for trademarks in every category you'll sell in.

## Getting started

Map every brand, product and range you have today, and the ones planned for the next two to three years. Group them by audience, price and channel, then choose the model that fits most of the portfolio.
